02 / Understand your options

You have rights.
Even when you’re overwhelmed.

These are the basics families most often miss.

01

Get the prices.

When you discuss arrangements or prices in person, you must be offered a General Price List to keep. Providers must also give price information over the phone.

02

Choose the pieces.

You can generally select individual goods and services. A nondeclinable basic services fee and legally required items may still apply.

03

Question “required.”

Embalming is not routinely required by law. If someone says it is necessary, ask whether that comes from law, the selected service, or the provider’s policy.

04

Ask for the other price lists.

If you ask about caskets or outer burial containers, the provider must give you a separate price list for each before showing you those items.

05

Bring your own casket.

A provider generally may not refuse to use a casket you bought elsewhere, and may not charge you a fee for using it.

06

Get it itemized in writing.

At the end of the arrangements conversation, you should receive a statement listing each good and service you chose and the total cost.

Read the FTC’s Funeral Rule guidance ↗. These protections apply to providers covered by the Rule. State requirements and your specific circumstances matter.

Ask what “required” means

A law, a policy, or a recommendation?

Understand requirements, alternatives, and prices ↗

When you’re arranging a funeral, those distinctions matter. Before agreeing to a charge or giving up an option, ask what the requirement is based on.

Required by law?

Ask the provider to identify the rule and explain why it applies to your arrangements. Check state-specific questions with the relevant regulator.

A funeral home’s policy?

Ask for the policy in writing, which services it affects, and what alternatives the provider offers. Another provider may have different policies.

A recommendation?

Ask why it is recommended, what it costs, and what happens if you decline. Explain your preferences and budget before choosing.

Told embalming is required for a viewing?

You can ask:

“Is that a legal requirement or your funeral home’s policy? If it’s required by law, can you show me the rule that applies? If it’s your policy, what viewing or visitation options are available without embalming, and what would they cost?”

Start with the FTC Funeral Rule consumer guide and your state’s resources.

If something doesn’t add up

Where to take a concern.

Start by asking the provider to explain in writing. If the answer is still unclear or you think a rule was broken, these are the places to go.

The FTC

The FTC enforces the Funeral Rule. You can report a concern at reportfraud.ftc.gov ↗.

Your state funeral board

Each state licenses funeral providers. Examples: North Carolina and South Carolina. For other states, use the USA.gov state directory.

Your state attorney general

Most state attorneys general take consumer protection complaints. Keep the price lists, the itemized statement, and any messages to attach.

A little clarity

The questions behind the bill.

Does an expensive funeral mean someone broke the law?

No. A high price or markup alone does not establish a Funeral Rule violation. The Rule concerns disclosures and sales practices; it does not set price caps. Compare equivalent services and document what you were told.

Can this site certify a price list as compliant?

No. This site offers educational information and a manual checklist. It does not assess uploaded documents, certify compliance, or determine whether a provider violated the law.

What information does this site collect?

This site has no account system, analytics scripts, cookies, or document uploads. Your answers to the two starting questions are placed in the address of the next page and may appear in hosting request logs. See the privacy page for details.

Why Morticians Audit exists

Experience in funeral service.
A purpose rooted in consumers.

From the founder

I’m a former North Carolina–licensed funeral director and embalmer. In my experience working in the industry, I saw sales take priority over honoring people and respecting families’ budgets. Those experiences conflicted with the reasons I entered funeral service, and they led me to leave. They inform this project; they do not describe every provider.

My guiding belief is that grief should never be treated as a sales opportunity. Families deserve room to choose, and thoughtful providers deserve recognition for helping them do that.

I created Morticians Audit to put consumers first—with clear information, practical help, and respect for their choices. Honoring someone you love should never require spending more than you can comfortably afford, or more than you choose to spend.

Useful help, without pressure.

This site’s guidance and manual checklist are free, with no account or payment required. The goal is to help you understand costs and ask informed questions at your own pace.

Trust through transparency.

Personal experience informs the questions we ask; linked official sources support the guidance. We distinguish experience from legal requirements, and we say so where state-specific guidance is still being researched. A high price alone is not proof of wrongdoing.

Covering costs, respecting choices.

The aim is to cover the cost of keeping this resource useful and available. There is currently nothing for sale here. If optional paid tools are introduced, their prices and limits will be explained upfront, while essential rights information and basic guidance remain free.

Fair to families. Fair to providers.

Funeral homes are businesses with real operating costs: staff, facilities, vehicles, equipment, and availability when families need them. Prices need to cover those costs and allow a sustainable profit. The purchase cost of an item alone does not explain the full cost of providing a service.

Our focus is transparent pricing, accurate explanations, and meaningful choices. A higher price or profit margin alone is not evidence of exploitation. Pressure, misleading claims about requirements, and unexplained charges deserve questions. Clear answers help families and conscientious providers work together.
